01 / RECOGNISEKNOW THE DIFFERENCE

NOT EVERY CONFLICT IS THE SAME

WHEN A
JOKE STOPS
BEING FUNNY.

01

A conversation can be awkward, unfair, or hurtful. Cyberbullying may involve repeated targeting online, while a single serious incident can also cause harm. Recognising the impact and pattern helps you respond.

Pause and ask: Is someone being targeted? Can they make it stop? Are others joining in?

A HISTORICAL SCHOOL BULLYING FINDING

UPSTANDERS
CAN MATTER.

In an earlier observational study of school bullying, 57% of episodes where peers intervened stopped within 10 seconds. It illustrates the potential of peers to interrupt harm.

Important: this was not an Australian cyberbullying trial, not a probability that any one action will work, and not a direct comparison with the prevalence chart.

Research and context ↗

Source: US StopBullying.gov, Bystanders Are Essential to Bullying Prevention and Intervention, referencing observational research on school bullying. The 57% applies to observed instances with peer intervention; it must not be generalized to online interactions.

THE GROUP CHAT IS NOT A COURTROOM

YOU DON'T

02

A bystander can help without escalating a situation. Don't forward humiliating content. Check on the person privately. If it is safe, show support or ask someone you trust to step in.

The strongest response isn't always the loudest one.

03 / SUPPORTTAKE A SAFER NEXT STEP

YOU DON'T HAVE TO FIX IT ALONE

EVIDENCE.

03

If cyberbullying happens, collect evidence such as screenshots or links, report harmful content on the platform, and speak with a trusted adult. Blocking or muting can help create space.

For practical Australian guidance, visit the eSafety Commissioner.
